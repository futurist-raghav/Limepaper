## 23.3 Investor & VC Outreach Strategy

### 23.3.1 Finding investors who actually fit this business

The single most common mistake a first-time manufacturing founder makes in outreach is defaulting to whatever VC list is most visible — which in India is overwhelmingly consumer-tech and SaaS seed funds. Those funds are structurally the wrong audience for Limepaper: they underwrite software-margin, software-velocity businesses (near-zero marginal cost, fast iteration cycles, asset-light balance sheets), and a capital-intensive plant business with 24-month commissioning timelines and physical collateral doesn't fit their model, regardless of how good the pitch is. Time spent pitching them is time not spent on the funds that actually understand plant economics.

The correct filter is sector-first, not brand-first:

* **Search by thesis language, not fund size.** Look for funds whose own materials use the words "deep tech," "industrial," "climate hardware," "circular economy," "materials science," or "decarbonisation" — not "consumer," "D2C," or "platform." A fund's own website and recent press releases (Inc42, Entrepreneur India, YourStory, Business Standard's startup desk) are the fastest way to check this before ever emailing.
* **Check actual portfolio composition, not stated thesis.** Many funds claim broad "deep tech" mandates but their actual cheques cluster in AI/software; verify by looking at 3–5 recent investments, not the About page.
* **Match check size to what's actually being raised.** A fund whose typical cheque is $5–10M is not a pre-seed conversation for a first Limepaper round — approaching them too early burns a relationship that would fit at Stage 2.
* **Weight funds with circular-economy or feedstock-adjacent portfolio companies higher.** A fund that has already backed a recycling, waste-management, or materials-substitution company understands the unit-economics vocabulary (yield, utilisation, feedstock security) that a generalist fund will not.

### 23.3.2 The physical-letter mechanic

VC inboxes are flooded with cold outreach; a well-targeted physical letter is a genuine differentiator precisely because almost no founder sends one. This only works for funds with a GST-registered Indian entity — most SEBI-registered AIFs and fund management LLPs are, since fund management fees are a taxable GST supply, but confirm this per fund before relying on it.

**To get a registered address:**

1. **Via the GST portal:** go to [services.gst.gov.in](https://services.gst.gov.in), use "Search Taxpayer" → "Search by GSTIN/UIN," and enter the fund entity's GSTIN if you have it (sometimes listed on invoices, MCA filings, or the fund's own website footer). The result returns the legal name and "Principal Place of Business" address, which is usually the registered office.
2. **Via LinkedIn:** a fund's LinkedIn company page "About" section frequently lists a registered office address directly, without needing a GSTIN — often the faster route in practice.
3. **Via the MCA portal (a reliable companion, not a substitute):** [mca.gov.in](https://www.mca.gov.in) lets you search a company or LLP by name and pull its registered office address from incorporation filings — worth cross-checking against the GST/LinkedIn result, since fund management entities are frequently structured as LLPs.

A short, specific letter — one page, addressed to a named partner (not "the investment team"), referencing their actual portfolio companies rather than a generic pitch — signals more effort than the volume of cold email a partner receives in a week, and is far more likely to get opened and remembered.

### 23.3.3 Biweekly update cadence once contact is made

Once a fund has responded or a first meeting has happened, keep them warm without becoming noise. A biweekly (every two weeks) email update, kept short, should contain exactly three things:

1. **One headline metric or milestone** — a signed LOI, a lab test result, a bank sanction milestone, a first sample run completed. If there is genuinely nothing new, skip that update rather than padding it.
2. **One honest challenge** — what's currently blocking progress or what didn't go as planned. This is not optional filler; investors who evaluate manufacturing businesses specifically read for whether a founder discloses problems honestly, since that's the same trait that determines whether they'll hear about a real problem in month eight of a funded plant.
3. **One specific ask** — an introduction to a potential anchor buyer, a vendor reference check, feedback on a specific number — never a vague "let us know if you can help." A specific, small ask is easy to say yes to and keeps the relationship active.

Do not over-promise timelines to sound impressive, and do not send updates more often than biweekly — a founder who emails weekly with marginal news reads as anxious, not fast-moving.
