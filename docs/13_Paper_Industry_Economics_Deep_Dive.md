## 13.1 The current situation, in one paragraph

India's conventional paper industry is a ≈23.8 million tonne/year market, running thin, cyclical EBITDA margins that just compressed sharply industry-wide — from ~20–29% at most listed players in FY24 to single digits (or losses) by FY25–26 — because its two biggest cost lines (recycled/waste paper, ~74% of furnish, mostly imported; and wood pulp/energy) are dollar-denominated or crude-linked, on top of import competition serious enough to trigger a 2026 anti-dumping case. Stone paper doesn't beat this industry on raw material price alone in India: Rajasthan's cheap limestone is real but small (~₹1,400–3,800/t), while HDPE (~₹17,000–22,000/t) is the dominant, equally volatile cost line. The "~40% cheaper capex" claim in earlier drafts holds only against *virgin wood-pulp* mills — against India's actual capex data (N R Agarwal's recycled-fibre board expansion ran ≈₹36,000/tonne vs. stone paper's own ≈₹121,000–123,000/tonne), stone paper is **considerably more capital-intensive per tonne** than the recycled-fibre packaging-board capacity that dominates India's real cost structure — worth carrying into any investor conversation. Stone paper's real advantages are narrower but genuine: ~0 water, ~55–75% less energy, and a differentiated performance story (waterproof, tear-resistant, non-toxic) conventional paper can't tell at any price. The market it sells into is small (India ≈USD 59–123m, 8.8% CAGR, vs. India's ≈23.8 million tonne conventional industry) and priced as a premium product (2–5x retail vs. conventional notebooks, ~2–3x per-kg vs. kraft), not a cost substitute. **You are not trying to out-cost JK Paper or ITC on capex or raw material, and cannot — you're selling a premium, differentiated substrate on water/energy footprint and physical performance, into buyers who already pay for compliance, durability, or brand story, at a scale small enough that ₹1.5 crore of collateral-backed capital can reach profitability — see §13.8 on why loan size was never what would have made the addressable market bigger.**

*Correction to an earlier estimate in this section: packaging's volume share is closer to **65%** than the 55–60% first estimated, and furnish is far more recycled-fibre-dominant (73–76%) than wood-based (18–20%) — the "conventional paper" India mostly makes is recycled-fibre packaging paper, not virgin wood-pulp writing paper.*

### 13.2.1 What it costs India's paper industry to make one tonne of conventional paper

No single "cost per tonne" — it varies by furnish (virgin wood pulp vs. recycled waste paper vs. agro-residue) and grade. Industry-wide cost-structure percentages (already cited in [03_Unit_Economics.md](03_Unit_Economics.md) §4.6.1) applied to a realistic all-in production cost:

| Cost element | % of total (recycled-fibre mill) | ₹/tonne (at ~₹45,000–55,000/t all-in, mid-tier writing/printing) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Raw material (pulp/wood/waste paper) | 55–65% | ₹25,000–36,000 |
| Utilities (power & fuel) | 20–25% | ₹9,000–13,750 |
| Employee cost | 5–8% | ₹2,250–4,400 |
| Chemicals (bleaching, sizing, coating) | 5–10% | ₹2,250–5,500 |
| Maintenance & other | 6–10% | ₹2,700–5,500 |
| **Total production cost per tonne** | 100% | **≈ ₹45,000–55,000** |

*A mid-tier writing/printing-paper cost base on recycled fibre (India's dominant furnish, mostly imported — domestic collection is weak). A virgin-wood-pulp mill (JK Paper, Century, Andhra Paper, all with captive plantations) runs a different, generally higher raw-material cost per §4.6.1 (₹32,000–60,000+/t for wood alone), partly offset by no import duty/freight on waste paper. Kraft/packaging grade runs meaningfully cheaper (simpler process, cheaper furnish, less bleaching/coating) — 2024–25 kraft ex-mill pricing of ₹32,500–33,000/t implies a packaging-grade cost base likely in the ₹25,000–35,000/t range, well below the writing/printing figure above.*

### 13.2.2 Input cost trends putting pressure on the industry right now

| Input | Trend | Source/date |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Domestic pulpwood | Risen ~88% (₹8,000/t → ₹15,000/t) recently | thepulpandpapertimes.com |
| Recovered/waste paper, European OCC benchmark | US $170–190/t (2024, down from $200+); ~$200/t stable through 2025 | thepulpandpapertimes.com; gtaic.ai, 2025 — ≈₹16,600–16,800/t [derived] |
| Waste paper import volumes | 5.756M tonnes, Jan–Oct 2025, **+19.33% YoY** — recovering from a 24.9% 2024 contraction; import value US $1,362.86m, Nov 2024–Oct 2025, +13.14% YoY | gtaic.ai, 2025 |
| GST on waste paper (HSN 4707) | Cut 12% → **5%**, effective 22 Sep 2025 | Multiple tax/logistics sources, 2025 |
| Global wood pulp (China net benchmark) | Softwood (NBSK) ≈$690/t (Aug 2025) → $690/t (Feb 2026); Hardwood (BHKP) ≈$495/t (Aug 2025) → $585/t (Feb 2026) | Fastmarkets/XP Research — softwood ≈₹57,300–58,000/t [derived] |
| Kraft paper index (India) | ~$420/t (≈₹35/kg), +0.31% QoQ | ChemAnalyst/Pulp & Paper Times, 2025 |
| Energy | 16–25% of production cost; coal/biomass boiler moves with fuel prices, electricity ~₹7.5/kWh | Industry commentary, 2025 |
| Anti-dumping duty on paperboard imports | DGTR final finding, 25 Jun 2026: US $261–376/t on virgin multi-layer paperboard from Indonesia, 5-yr duration — IPMA claimed ₹242cr in losses over Apr 2024–Mar 2025 | thepulpandpapertimes.com, Jun 2026 |
| Net effect | Margins compress when pulp/waste-paper/energy prices spike faster than pass-through to price-sensitive converters — while fighting import competition significant enough to trigger a 2025–26 anti-dumping case | Synthesis |

**The honest read:** conventional mills fight a structurally rising, commodity-linked cost base with a price-sensitive customer base resisting pass-through — why the large players below run thin, cyclical margins despite scale. That's the industry stone paper sits alongside, not underneath. India doesn't produce meaningful market softwood pulp domestically — large integrated mills (JK Paper, Andhra Paper, Century/ITC, TNPL, Seshasayee) rely on captive plantation wood, partly insulating them from global pulp benchmarks; smaller converters are more exposed.

### 13.3.2 The pattern that matters more than any single company's number

Nearly every listed player shows **sharp EBITDA margin compression from FY24 to FY25/FY26** — JK Paper 24%→13%, Andhra Paper 24%→4%, TNPL 16%→10%, Kuantum 29%→15%, Satia 24%→9% (net losses by Q1 FY27), Orient swinging to consecutive operating losses. **Industry-wide, not company-specific** — driven by rising wood/pulp costs, low-priced paperboard imports (§13.2.2), and soft realizations mills can't fully pass through. Benchmark any stone-paper case against this **compressed FY25/FY26 baseline**, not the unusually strong FY24 year — FY24 margins would overstate current industry profitability by roughly half.

These bracket ₹36,000–91,000/tonne depending on grade/furnish complexity — context for [03_Unit_Economics.md](03_Unit_Economics.md) §4.3's stone-paper benchmarks (≈₹17.2cr/1,400 t/yr ≈ ₹123,000/tonne at EIRI scale; ≈₹60.5cr/5,000 t/yr ≈ ₹121,000/tonne at global mid-scale). **Stone paper's capex-per-tonne at these scales does not look cheaper than a conventional recycled-fibre board expansion — it looks considerably more expensive.** The "40% cheaper than wood-pulp" claim ([03_Unit_Economics.md](03_Unit_Economics.md) §4.6.2) holds specifically against *virgin wood-pulp* mills, not India's dominant recycled-fibre furnish — flag this correction for investor/lender pitches. Stone paper's real advantages remain water (~0 vs ~200t/tonne) and energy (~700kWh vs ~2,908kWh/tonne), not capex efficiency, against India's actual cost structure.

## 13.5 Stone paper vs. conventional paper — the real comparison, side by side

| Dimension | Conventional (wood-pulp/recycled) paper | Stone paper | Who wins, and why it matters |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Raw material cost/tonne | ₹25,000–36,000 (55–65% of total cost) | ₹19,000–27,000 ([03_Unit_Economics.md](03_Unit_Economics.md) §4.2) | Stone paper — but the gap is smaller than headline "cheap limestone" framing suggests, since HDPE is expensive |
| Water/tonne | ~200 tonnes | ~0 | Stone paper, decisively — a structural, not marginal, advantage in water-stressed Rajasthan |
| Energy/tonne | ~2,908 kWh | ~700 kWh (76% less) | Stone paper — pairs with captive solar for a real cost + carbon-story advantage |
| Capex per tonne of capacity | ₹36,000/t (N R Agarwal, actual, §13.3.3) to ₹91,000/t (Orient Paper, actual) | ₹121,000–123,000/t (EIRI/global benchmarks) | **Conventional (recycled-fibre) paper** — "~40% cheaper" only holds vs. virgin wood-pulp mills, not India's actual recycled-fibre base. Correct this in investor materials. |
| Ex-factory/wholesale price | ₹32,500–55,000/t by grade | ₹60,000–108,000+/t by channel | Conventional — stone paper isn't price-competitive on a like-for-like commodity basis |
| Retail finished-goods price | Baseline | 2–5x premium (notebooks), ~2–3x per-kg (packaging) | Neither "wins" — different market; stone paper sells on performance/ESG, not cost |
| Input volatility | Wood +88% recently; imported waste paper subject to global collection/shipping swings | HDPE swung ₹13,000/t (≈11%) in 9 days, Jul 2026 | Roughly comparable — both volatile, differently |
| Regulatory tailwind | Digitization headwind on writing/printing; packaging has the same SUP-ban tailwind as stone paper | SUP ban + EPR rules directly favor plastic-alternative substrates; GST/HSN genuinely unresolved (§13.6) | Stone paper has a clearer packaging-side tailwind, but real GST classification risk conventional paper doesn't carry |
| Recyclability/end-of-life | Well-established infrastructure | Limited HDPE-mineral composite recycling infrastructure in India — a real credibility risk ([02_Market_Opportunity.md](02_Market_Opportunity.md) §3.4) | Conventional paper — stone paper's "tree-free" story is undercut without a proactive end-of-life answer |

**Bottom line:** stone paper doesn't win a commodity cost fight against conventional Indian paper, and shouldn't try — not on raw material, and, contrary to earlier framing here, not on capex either against India's actual recycled-fibre-dominant base (§13.3.3). It wins narrowly but genuinely on water, energy, and a performance/durability story sellable as a premium, differentiated product to buyers who value those things — a smaller but real, underserved market (India's stone paper market ≈USD 59–123m vs. a ≈23.8 million tonne conventional industry). The ₹1.5cr ceiling in [07_Capital_Plan.md](07_Capital_Plan.md) is sized for a slice of that smaller premium market, not to compete head-on with JK Paper or ITC on tonnage or capital efficiency — see §13.8.

## 13.6 Regulatory and tax factors that materially affect the economics

| Factor | Status | Why it matters |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| GST/HSN classification of stone paper | **Unresolved in public sources.** Conventional paper (Chapter 48): 5% (kraft/copier) to 12% (uncoated paper/paperboard, tissue). Stone paper (CaCO3/HDPE composite) could plausibly be Chapter 48 (5–12%), HSN 3921 (plastic sheet/film, 18%), or Chapter 68 (articles of stone) | A 5–12% vs. 18% swing is a material margin difference and affects input-tax-credit eligibility. **Get a formal GST advance ruling before finalizing pricing** — the single highest-priority regulatory confirmation from this research. |
| Single-Use Plastics (SUP) Rules | In force since 1 Jul 2022; 2025 rules extend stricter enforcement; from 1 Jul 2025, plastic bags/multilayer packaging need barcode/QR traceability | Direct demand tailwind for plastic-alternative substrates — but enforcement is uneven, and paper/compostable substitutes reportedly cost more than the plastics replaced, causing small-trader resistance |
| Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) | Recycled-content mandates in force for 2025–26; targets 40–60% (rising to 50–70% by 2026–27) by category; penalties ₹10,000–₹1.5m + ₹10,000/day continuing | Creates a compliance-driven buyer (packaging companies hitting EPR targets) distinct from ESG-voluntary buyers |
| Corporate ESG commitments | ITC (100% recyclable/reusable/biodegradable by 2028, plastic-neutral 4 yrs running), HUL (100% by 2030, acquired Lucro Plastecycle Mar 2025), Marico (100% by 2025, PVC elimination) | Primary-packaging commitments at large-brand scale, not directly addressable by a bootstrapped entrant — but they create an internal ESG procurement culture receptive to smaller orders (corporate gifting, collateral) — the actually-addressable opportunity |
| Rajasthan-specific policy | RIPS 2024–2029 and Circular Economy Incentive Scheme 2025 ([03_Unit_Economics.md](03_Unit_Economics.md) §4.5) | No change — still the standout state-level fit given the ≥51%-recycled-input threshold |
| GeM green procurement | Explicit "discourages plastic, encourages green procurement" policy; ₹18.4 lakh crore cumulative GMV | A channel to build toward (register, target state stationery tenders), not a proven near-term source — GeM tenders are typically L1 (lowest-price)-driven unless green-scoring genuinely offsets that; needs direct verification |

## 13.7 Market capture strategy — ranked target sectors

Extends [09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md](09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md) §11.1 with sizing and willingness-to-pay evidence, reordered based on what the research actually supports.

**1. Corporate gifting & premium diaries/notebooks (B2B, ESG-branded) — lead with this.** Fastest path to first revenue, no certification barrier, low working-capital exposure, exploits a proven willingness to pay: Indian retail already accepts a 50–150%+ premium for eco-notebooks (₹70–300+ vs. ₹40–80 conventional) when the buyer is a company purchasing for CSR/ESG optics. The India Plastics Pact's 27 founding members (Tata Consumer, HUL, ITC, Godrej, Marico, Coca-Cola India, Mondelez, others) are exactly the corporate buyer universe whose marketing/HR/CSR departments need branded, plastic-free-story gifting SKUs. Also the segment Ston2pack proved demand for via Whitestone Stationeries before going dormant ([04_Competitive_Landscape.md](04_Competitive_Landscape.md) §5.4) — a warm category, not an unproven one.

**2. Pharma secondary packaging & cartons — highest structural fit, pursue in parallel.** India's pharma packaging market (US $3.4–5.2bn, 2025, 8.7–20% CAGR) is trending toward paperboard for secondary cartons because water-based coatings now achieve 95% humidity resistance — pharma buyers already pay for the exact property (moisture resistance) stone paper offers natively without coating. Compliance- and quality-driven rather than price-driven; Rajasthan/Gujarat's pharma corridor is a natural proximity advantage. Caveat: verify FSSAI/pharma-grade certification for HDPE content before pitching — a real gate, not a formality.

**3. Industrial labels, tags & synthetic-paper niche (menus, ID cards, outdoor signage, hang tags) — highest margin.** The one segment where waterproof/tear-resistant properties are the *entire* purchase criterion. India's synthetic paper market is served almost entirely by PVC/PP players (Cosmo Films, Tirupati Marketing) — stone paper offers genuine plastic-free differentiation against a 100%-petroleum-plastic incumbent. Adidas's global use of stone paper for shoe hang tags is a proven, exportable use case. Lower volume than packaging, best margin-per-tonne of any segment identified.

**4. E-commerce mailers/labels — large market, approach selectively, not as a volume play.** Deprioritized vs. [09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md](09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md)'s original #2: poly mailers cost ₹1.30–6/piece, structurally 30–50% cheaper than any paper alternative — a pure cost-parity market at Amazon/Flipkart/Meesho scale (Meesho alone: 2.02 billion orders to Jun 2025) that a bootstrapped entrant cannot win on price at any Stage 1 loan size. **Refine, don't abandon:** target premium D2C brands (skincare, fashion, gifting-adjacent) wanting a tactile "unboxing" sustainability story who already pay up for packaging as brand marketing — not the marketplaces' own high-volume RFPs.

**5. Government/institutional (GeM, Rajasthan state departments) — build toward, don't lead with.** Rajasthan's SPCB has internal SUP elimination orders and GeM has an explicit green-procurement policy — real signals, but no specific tender or PSU mandate for stone-paper stationery/packaging was found. Develop over the plan's first 24 months (GeM registration, direct outreach citing Rajasthan's own mandate), not early revenue.

**6. Food packaging (grease-resistant: bakery, QSR) — deprioritize initially.** India's fastest-growing greaseproof-paper market (8.1% CAGR) is already served by established cellulose-based players with proven FSSAI food-contact certification — harder, slower entry for a capital-constrained new entrant. Revisit as a secondary, non-direct-food-contact application (bakery box liners) once quality and certifications are proven elsewhere.

**Net sequencing:** #1 (corporate gifting) is the fastest route to revenue against a commissioned Stage 1 line ([07_Capital_Plan.md](07_Capital_Plan.md) §9.2), and most likely to yield the pre-commissioning LOIs that gate capex; #2 (pharma) and #3 (industrial labels) are higher-value relationships to build in parallel once lab certification exists (Phase B, [08_Implementation_Roadmap.md](08_Implementation_Roadmap.md)); #4 stays opportunistic and brand-selective; #5 and #6 are 18–24 month horizon, not day-one targets.

## 13.8 Your stance — what this deep-dive actually changes vs. confirms

* **Confirms — and the same-day reversion from ₹3cr back to ₹1.5cr is the right call on this document's own logic, not just underwriting:** ₹1.5cr was, and remains, the right order of magnitude for a bootstrapped Stage 1 in a market this small (India's stone paper category ≈USD 59–123m). The brief attempt to raise the ceiling to ₹3cr was justified by a larger extrusion line and a second converting machine, **not by a bigger market that had appeared** — the USD 59–123m size never moved. That mismatch (more capacity chasing a same-size market) was one reason ₹3cr didn't hold up; ₹1.5cr's capacity fits this market better, collateral or not. The ₹15cr Stage 2 raise (§9.3) is underwritten against the same USD 59–123m market, so the real sizing question is "does ≈USD 59–123m support a ₹150cr post-money valuation" — a hard question this document doesn't answer in the affirmative, regardless of how Stage 1 is financed. Confirms the B2B-first, corporate-gifting-led sequencing already in [04_Competitive_Landscape.md](04_Competitive_Landscape.md) §5.3 and [09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md](09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md).
* **Sharpens:** don't sell raw stone paper as an undifferentiated substrate — Chinese export FOB pricing (~₹94,000–108,000/t) sets a brutal floor for a commodity supplier. Sell finished, branded, differentiated product where that floor doesn't apply.
* **Adds a genuinely new risk to track:** the GST/HSN question (§13.6) is unresolved and could swing effective margin by 6–13 percentage points — belongs in [05_Risks_Diligence.md](05_Risks_Diligence.md) as an open item, resolved via formal advance ruling before Stage 2 capital, not discovered at first sale.
* **Reprioritizes:** pharma secondary packaging deserves to sit alongside corporate gifting as a parallel early target, not wait behind e-commerce mailers — e-commerce mailer willingness-to-pay at marketplace scale is actually weak (structurally cost-competitive against poly), while pharma's moisture-resistance trend is a real, current buying criterion.
* **Confirms the core discipline already in the plan:** stone paper is not a cheaper-than-wood-pulp play in India. HDPE erases most of the limestone cost advantage, and finished stone paper sells at a 2–5x retail premium globally and in India. The plan only works with premium positioning — chasing cost parity with JK Paper or Chinese import stone paper is not the fight to pick, at ₹1.5cr or otherwise.
* **Genuinely revises a prior claim — don't carry forward uncorrected:** [03_Unit_Economics.md](03_Unit_Economics.md) §4.6.2 cites stone paper as "~40% lower capex" than a conventional mill, from a generic global comparison. Real Indian capex data (§13.3.3 — N R Agarwal ≈₹36,000/tonne, Orient Paper ≈₹91,000/tonne) shows this holds only against *virgin wood-pulp* mills, which aren't most of India's industry (73–76% is recycled fibre, §13.2). Against the recycled-fibre capacity that actually dominates, stone paper's own benchmarks (≈₹121,000–123,000/tonne) run **higher**, not lower. Doesn't kill the plan — it means lenders/investors should hear the water/energy footprint case (still genuinely superior) and drop the capex-advantage claim, or qualify it explicitly as "vs. virgin wood-pulp mills only."
