# Vendor, Hiring & Capital Due Diligence — Protecting the Money, the Team, and the Decisions

*Part of the Limepaper business plan — see [00_README_Index.md](00_README_Index.md) for the full document set. This doc exists for one reason: at three of you, two of whom are 18, every vendor, consultant, contractor, and candidate you meet knows they're dealing with first-time buyers. This is the checklist that closes that gap. Pair with [11_Learning_Resources.md](11_Learning_Resources.md) — literacy and process discipline work together; neither alone is enough.*

## 12.1 Machinery vendor due diligence (Stage 1 and Stage 2 equipment)

This applies to every equipment purchase — the ₹6–12 lakh Stage 1 converting machine and, at much higher stakes, the Stage 2 extrusion line ([07_Capital_Plan.md](07_Capital_Plan.md)).

### Before you ever send money

* **Verify the company is real and registered**, independent of whatever IndiaMART/TradeIndia listing you found them through: GST registration, MCA (Ministry of Corporate Affairs) company search, and — if they claim to export/import — an IEC (Import Export Code). All of these are checkable yourself, free, on government portals, in minutes.
* **Search the company name, directors' names, and phone number together with "fraud," "complaint," "cheated," "scam."** Look for prior disputes, recovery notices, or buyers describing a pattern that matches what you're seeing.
* **Confirm the factory address is a real industrial premises**, not a residential flat or a shared virtual office, using satellite/street-view imagery and a direct call to a landline (not just a mobile/WhatsApp number).
* **No physical, visitable address, or "we only communicate on WhatsApp"** is on its own a serious red flag — a genuine manufacturer has an address you can visit or have independently audited.

### The site visit itself (never skip this for a Stage 2-scale purchase, and strongly prefer it for Stage 1)

* **Count the machines against what you were quoted**, and notice whether equipment looks in regular productive use or staged for your visit.
* **Ask when key equipment was purchased and ask to see maintenance records** — a machine that's supposedly always been reliable but has no maintenance log at all is worth questioning.
* **Count the workers on the floor and compare against the production volumes they claim** — inconsistency here is a real tell.
* **Inspect the raw-material storage area** — genuine ongoing production leaves visible stock; an empty or token storage area alongside big production claims doesn't add up.
* **Check power backup and (if relevant) water treatment infrastructure** — claimed industrial-scale output needs the utilities to match.
* **Watch it actually run**, not just sit there. Ask for a live demo with your own material if at all possible, not just a pre-recorded video.

### Red flags to walk away from

* Factory is "under renovation" or the visit gets rescheduled repeatedly.
* Equipment present but visibly not operational, dusty, or clearly not the model discussed.
* Workers who don't know basic specifics of what they're supposedly producing.
* Only photocopied certificates, no originals available to inspect on-site.
* Factory address doesn't match the GST/MCA registration address.
* Any request for **100% payment in advance** — a genuine vendor selling real equipment almost always accepts a staged structure (see below).

### Payment structure — never pay it all upfront

* Standard, defensible structure: a deposit to confirm the order (commonly 20–30%), a further tranche on pre-shipment inspection or factory acceptance test (FAT), and a final tranche only on successful installation and commissioning at your site (SAT — site acceptance test). Negotiate toward this; a vendor who refuses any staging at all, especially for a first-time buyer, is a red flag in itself.
* **For any Stage 2-scale purchase (lakhs to crores), get an independent third-party inspection** before final payment — agencies like SGS, Bureau Veritas, Intertek, or AsiaInspection do this professionally, typically for a few hundred to low-thousands of dollars — genuinely cheap insurance relative to the amount at risk.
* Put warranty, AMC (annual maintenance contract) terms, spares availability, and training commitments **in the written contract**, not as verbal promises made during the sales pitch.

## 12.2 Feedstock supplier due diligence

Specific to marble slurry / CaCO3 suppliers, given the purity issues flagged in [03_Unit_Economics.md](03_Unit_Economics.md) §4.2.1 and [05_Risks_Diligence.md](05_Risks_Diligence.md):

* **Lab-test every new source before signing an offtake agreement — and re-test periodically after**, not just the first sample. A supplier who resists ongoing testing, or whose quality drifts once a contract is signed, is a known failure pattern in commodity sourcing generally.
* **Get purity/whiteness/particle-size specs written into the supply agreement itself**, with a rejection/return clause for out-of-spec batches — not just a verbal understanding.
* **Prefer suppliers with an existing, checkable track record** (ask for other customers you can call) over the cheapest quote from an unknown source, especially for your first 6–12 months while you're still calibrating your own QC process.

## 12.3 Hiring & team due diligence

Applies to every technical hire — process engineer, converting-line technician, QC specialist ([06_Core_Team_Experts.md](06_Core_Team_Experts.md)) — and to any consultant or contractor.

* **Verify claimed credentials directly**, not just from a resume: call the institute for a diploma/ITI certificate, call a listed previous employer rather than only checking a written reference letter.
* **Use a paid skills test or short trial task before a full hire**, especially for machine-operator roles — someone who claims PLC-machine experience should be able to demonstrate it on the actual (or a similar) machine, not just describe it.
* **Never pay a candidate's "training fee" or "certification fee" upfront** — this is a well-known pattern used against inexperienced employers; a genuine hire's training cost is yours to bear as an employer, not something you pay the candidate to unlock.
* **Put everything in writing from day one:** an offer letter with role, compensation, and probation terms; an NDA and IP-assignment clause for anyone touching your process knowledge or supplier relationships (this matters more than usual here — your feedstock-supplier relationship is explicitly called out as your moat in [06_Core_Team_Experts.md](06_Core_Team_Experts.md) §8.2); a notice period.
* **For any consultant (DPR, compliance, legal, CA) — check they're independently engaged by you, not recommended-and-controlled by the same vendor whose equipment or scheme they're advising you on.** A "free" DPR consultant who only ever recommends one machinery vendor is not actually independent.

## 12.4 Capital & contract due diligence

* **Never sign a personal guarantee, loan document, or supplier contract without reading every clause yourself** — even with a CA or lawyer present, you (and your father, per [06_Core_Team_Experts.md](06_Core_Team_Experts.md) §8.0) are the one legally bound, and you need to understand exactly what happens if the business can't repay.
* **Use your own lawyer, not the counterparty's.** If a vendor, landlord, or investor offers to "handle the paperwork" or recommends "their guy" for legal review, that's a conflict of interest — get [06_Core_Team_Experts.md](06_Core_Team_Experts.md) role #4 (independent legal counsel) to review anything before signing, every time, no exceptions for "it's just a formality."
* **Understand CGTMSE correctly:** the guarantee covers the *bank*, not you — it makes the bank willing to lend without collateral, but you remain personally liable for repayment. Don't let a bank officer's "it's collateral-free" framing make you think the debt itself is somehow lower-risk.
* **Hold the line on the ₹1.5 crore ceiling** ([07_Capital_Plan.md](07_Capital_Plan.md) §9.1) against anyone — vendor, bank, or well-meaning advisor — who suggests "just borrow a bit more, it's not a big difference." It is a big difference to what you can personally service and guarantee.

## 12.5 A standing discipline, not a one-time checklist

* **Keep a shared running log** (even a simple spreadsheet) of every vendor/supplier/hire due-diligence check performed, who did it, and what was found — across three people, an informal "someone probably checked that" is exactly how gaps happen.
* **Slow down specifically when someone is pushing urgency** ("this price is only good today," "sign now or lose the slot") — urgency pressure is one of the most consistent tells across vendor and hiring fraud generally, independent of industry.
* **Revisit this checklist before every new category of spend** — Stage 1 equipment, feedstock contracts, Stage 2 machinery, your first real hires, and again before any Stage 2 loan documents are signed.
