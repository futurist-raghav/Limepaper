# Customer Knowledge Playbook — Know the Buyer Before You Pitch Them

*Part of the Limepaper business plan — see [00_README_Index.md](00_README_Index.md). Added 11 Sep 2026.*

**What this doc is and isn't.** The founder's brief was "know your customer in and out — the right info to the right person, from mindset to relationship with technology." Everything below is a working hypothesis assembled from the market/segment research already in [02_Market_Opportunity.md](02_Market_Opportunity.md), [09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md](09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md) and [13_Paper_Industry_Economics_Deep_Dive.md](13_Paper_Industry_Economics_Deep_Dive.md), **not from a single customer interview.** No B2B procurement officer and no D2C buyer has talked to this team yet. Treat every claim in §21.1–21.3 as "our best guess, to be broken by real conversations" — §21.4 is the plan to go break it, and it has to happen before Stage 1 equipment is ordered, same discipline as the LOI gate in [07_Capital_Plan.md](07_Capital_Plan.md) §9.2.4.

Two structurally different customers buy under the Limepaper name — same split as [09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md](09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md) §11.0's two channels. This doc is the "who are they, really" companion to that doc's "how do we reach them" playbook.

## 21.1 Persona 1 — the B2B packaging/procurement buyer

This is not one job title — "packaging converter," "FMCG procurement," "pharma packaging buyer" and "e-commerce ops" are different roles with different scorecards. The table below is written for the most common near-term target in [09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md](09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md) §11.1's priority order: a **packaging/procurement manager at a converter or mid-size brand**, evaluating a new substrate supplier. Where pharma or e-commerce buyers diverge materially, it's called out inline.

| Attribute | Detail |
| :---- | :---- |
| Typical title | Packaging Procurement Manager / Sourcing Manager / Materials Purchase Manager, sometimes "Sustainability & Packaging Lead" at larger FMCG accounts |
| What they're actually measured on | Cost-per-unit landed (not ex-factory quote — freight, MOQ, payment terms all count), on-time supply reliability, and increasingly an ESG/sustainability scorecard metric tied to their own company's EPR or public commitments ([13_Paper_Industry_Economics_Deep_Dive.md](13_Paper_Industry_Economics_Deep_Dive.md) §13.6). Rarely measured on "tried something innovative" — a failed new-supplier trial is a mark against them, not a badge of initiative |
| Reports to / influenced by | Category head or plant manager on cost and reliability; a separate sustainability/CSR function (where one exists) on the ESG line — these two internal stakeholders can want different things from the same supplier, and a small vendor has to satisfy both, not just whoever answers the phone |
| Relationship with technology | Not digitally native in *sourcing behaviour* — doesn't discover new suppliers on Instagram or via cold outbound email. Uses IndiaMART/TradeIndia search, trade-show floor conversations, existing-vendor referrals, and increasingly a formal e-procurement/RFQ portal at larger accounts. Comfortable with WhatsApp for day-to-day supplier communication once a relationship exists — the "not digital" read is about discovery, not workflow tooling once engaged |
| What info they need to move forward | (1) Tear-resistance and water-resistance numbers from a third-party lab, not a claim on a slide; (2) GST/HSN classification certainty — a buyer cannot commit to a supplier whose product might reclassify from 5–12% paper to 18% plastic-sheet mid-relationship ([13_Paper_Industry_Economics_Deep_Dive.md](13_Paper_Industry_Economics_Deep_Dive.md) §13.6); (3) ESG/sustainability certification status **stated honestly**, including the microplastic/end-of-life caveat below — a sophisticated buyer will ask directly, and answering only when asked reads worse than volunteering it first |
| Buying process | Sample request → internal lab/quality check against spec → small pilot order → supplier audit (factory visit or questionnaire) for a first-time vendor above a certain order value → approved-vendor listing → repeat purchase orders. Multiple sign-offs (procurement + quality + sometimes finance) even for a modest order value |
| Typical sales-cycle length, new substrate supplier | Realistically 3–9 months for a first qualifying order at a mid-size converter; 12+ months at a large FMCG/pharma account with a formal vendor-qualification process. [09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md](09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md) §11.2–11.3's "3–5 signed LOIs before equipment ordered" target assumes this cycle is already underway well before the plant exists — LOIs are a pre-qualification signal, not a finished sale |
| Likely objections | Ex-factory price above commodity wood-pulp paper in price-sensitive segments ([02_Market_Opportunity.md](02_Market_Opportunity.md) §3.4); "unproven small supplier, no track record" — a real objection for a two-founder, first-year plant, not a bias to argue away; recycling-stream/end-of-life concerns once they look past the pitch; capacity/reliability doubt — can a single Stage 1 line actually supply at the volume and consistency a repeat-order relationship needs |
| What proof actually convinces them | In order of weight: (1) physical samples they can test themselves, (2) third-party lab data (tear strength, water resistance, GSM-equivalent) on one page, per [09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md](09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md) §11.2, (3) a reference account — even one — who has already qualified and reordered, (4) a factory visit or at minimum photos/video of a real production run. A pitch deck alone convinces no one in this persona; [09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md](09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md) §11.2 already states this ("lead with lab data, not a pitch deck") and this persona is the reason why |

**21.1.1 The honest disclosure point.** A sophisticated ESG-sensitive buyer — the ITC/HUL-type account with a public "100% biodegradable/recyclable by 2028–2030" commitment ([13_Paper_Industry_Economics_Deep_Dive.md](13_Paper_Industry_Economics_Deep_Dive.md) §13.6) — is exactly the buyer with the technical staff to ask the right follow-up question about end-of-life. [16_Process_Environmental_Profile.md](16_Process_Environmental_Profile.md) §16.4 is unambiguous: the finished product is **not biodegradable, not compostable, and not accepted in standard paper- or plastic-recycling streams**; the ~18% HDPE fraction UV-photodegrades over 14–18 months into a CaCO₃ shell plus HDPE microplastic fragments. "Tree-free" and "water-free" are defensible and should be the headline claims (§16.5). "Plastic-free" is not defensible and is a real greenwashing exposure if this buyer discovers it later rather than being told upfront — [14_Founder_Reality_Check.md](14_Founder_Reality_Check.md) already flags this as a wording discipline to enforce everywhere. **For this specific persona, "later" means during their own lab/quality review, not after a signed contract** — so the right sequencing is to volunteer the caveat in the same conversation as the lab data, not to wait for the objection. A buyer who hears it from us first treats it as due diligence; a buyer who finds it themselves treats it as concealment, and that account becomes a negative reference, not just a lost sale.

**21.1.2 Where this persona diverges by sub-segment.**

| Sub-segment | Divergence from the base persona |
| :---- | :---- |
| Pharma secondary packaging ([13_Paper_Industry_Economics_Deep_Dive.md](13_Paper_Industry_Economics_Deep_Dive.md) §13.7 #2) | Adds a quality/compliance function to the sign-off chain (not just procurement); moisture resistance is already a paid-for spec via water-based coatings, so the "why pay more" objection is weaker here — the pitch is closer to "get what you already pay a coating step for, natively" |
| E-commerce mailer/label buyers | Ops-driven, more numeric, faster cycle if a marketplace-scale RFP — but [13_Paper_Industry_Economics_Deep_Dive.md](13_Paper_Industry_Economics_Deep_Dive.md) §13.7 #4 already flags that this buyer is structurally price-anchored to poly mailers (₹1.30–6/piece) and cannot be won on cost; only the premium-D2C-brand sub-slice (buying packaging as brand marketing, not logistics) fits this persona well |
| Industrial labels/tags converters | Smallest, most technical buyer — evaluates almost entirely on the property sheet (waterproof, tear-resistant) since their own competitive set is PVC/PP synthetic paper, not wood-pulp. Fastest-converting of the sub-segments because the ESG story matters less than the spec match |

## 21.2 Persona 2 — the D2C/retail finished-goods customer

This is the buyer for the ten branded SKUs in [18_Product_SKU_Catalog.md](18_Product_SKU_Catalog.md) — notebooks, premium carry bags, cartons, tags, menus/signage — sold under the Limepaper name via the storefront/marketplace motion in [08_Implementation_Roadmap.md](08_Implementation_Roadmap.md) Phase F. A completely different mindset from §21.1, and treating them the same way (a spec sheet and a lab-data one-pager) would be a wasted pitch.

| Attribute | Detail |
| :---- | :---- |
| Who they actually are | Two distinct sub-buyers, not one: (a) an individual retail/gifting shopper buying notebooks, diaries, small carry bags for personal use or as a gift; (b) a small-business or corporate-gifting buyer sourcing branded stationery/bags in bulk for CSR/ESG-flavoured gifting — closer to §21.1's mindset but at a much lower order threshold and shorter cycle |
| What drives the purchase | Story and sustainability first, spec second. [18_Product_SKU_Catalog.md](18_Product_SKU_Catalog.md) §18.2 and §18.11 already show this in the pricing data: stone-paper notebooks sell at a **2–5x retail premium** over conventional notebooks purely on brand/ESG positioning, not because the underlying material is cheaper to produce — the opposite is true (§18.1.2) |
| Price sensitivity | Genuinely two-tier, not uniform. At the basic-SKU tier (a ruled notebook, a simple tag) the buyer compares against a ₹40–150 Classmate/Navneet-tier product and is price-sensitive in absolute terms even while accepting a premium multiple. At the premium tier (a hardcover notebook, a bridal/gift carry bag) the buyer is paying for the object and the story as much as the function, and price sensitivity drops sharply — [18_Product_SKU_Catalog.md](18_Product_SKU_Catalog.md) §18.9 shows carry bags command real premiums at wedding/festive/gifting occasions specifically because the story ("made from Kishangarh's marble waste," waterproof, tear-resistant) carries weight there |
| Buying triggers | Personal gifting occasions (weddings, festivals — §18.9.1's "bridal bag" SKU exists because of this), corporate/CSR gifting cycles (Diwali hampers, onboarding kits), a specific "I want a sustainable stationery option" search intent, and impulse/discovery via social content — not a recurring planned procurement cycle like §21.1 |
| Where they're reached | Instagram and other visual social platforms (product-in-use photography, the "made from marble waste" origin story as content, not a spec sheet), a D2C storefront (Shopify/WooCommerce per [08_Implementation_Roadmap.md](08_Implementation_Roadmap.md) Phase F step 27), Amazon/Flipkart listings once volume justifies it, and — for the corporate-gifting sub-buyer specifically — direct outreach or referral, closer to a light version of §21.1's channel. **Not** trade shows, IndiaMART, or cold B2B sales calls — those are §21.1's channels and would waste effort reaching this buyer |
| Relationship with technology | Digitally native in the opposite direction from §21.1 — discovered and converted online, expects visual content, reviews, and an easy checkout, not a quote-and-sample cycle. A generic-looking storefront or a plain product photo undersells the story this buyer is actually paying for |
| What convinces them | Photography and material feel (this is a physical-product, tactile purchase — texture and the "it doesn't feel like ordinary paper" moment matters), a clear and honest sustainability story, social proof (reviews, influencer/gifting-occasion content), and price framed against a comparable premium product (Moleskine-tier notebooks, laminated-paper gift bags — [18_Product_SKU_Catalog.md](18_Product_SKU_Catalog.md) §18.2, §18.9.2), not against the cheapest commodity option |

**21.2.1 The same honesty point, different stakes.** §21.1.1's biodegradability caveat applies here too, but the risk shape is different. A D2C buyer is far less likely to ask a technical question before purchase — but far more likely to complain publicly (a review, a social post) if they bought on a "sustainable"/"eco-friendly" framing and later learn the product isn't biodegradable or recyclable. [16_Process_Environmental_Profile.md](16_Process_Environmental_Profile.md) §16.4's honest claims — tree-free, water-free, marble-waste-diverted — are still a genuinely strong retail story and should stay the headline (per §16.5's "net read"). The discipline is the same as §21.1.1: never let product copy say "plastic-free," "biodegradable," or "recyclable" — say what's actually true, on the product page itself, not buried in a footnote a reviewer has to go find.

## 21.3 The tech-mindset spectrum — why one pitch deck doesn't serve both

The two personas above sit at opposite ends of a "how do you find and evaluate a new supplier/product" spectrum, and that has to change more than the words in a pitch — it has to change the *format* of what's shown to each.

| | Persona 1 — B2B procurement buyer | Persona 2 — D2C retail buyer |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Discovery channel | IndiaMART/TradeIndia, trade shows, referral, direct sales call | Instagram/social, storefront/marketplace search, gifting-occasion browsing |
| Evaluation basis | Lab data, spec sheet, sample tested in-house, vendor audit | Photography, story, reviews, feel-in-hand |
| Decision unit | Multiple internal sign-offs (procurement, quality, sometimes finance/sustainability) | Individual or small-business buyer, single decision-maker |
| Cycle length | Months (3–9 typical, 12+ at large accounts) | Minutes to days |
| What "proof" means | Third-party lab certification, reference account, factory visit | Product photos, material feel, social proof, price-vs-comparable framing |
| Right sales artifact | A one-page technical spec sheet with lab numbers, a sample kit, a reference-account case — not a glossy deck ([09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md](09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md) §11.2 already says this) | A visual, story-led storefront/product page and social content — a spec sheet would undersell and actively bore this buyer |

**What this settles for the rest of the plan.** [14_Founder_Reality_Check.md](14_Founder_Reality_Check.md) §3 talks about consistent wording ("tree-free, water-free" not "plastic-free") across "every sales collateral, packaging, and pitch deck" — that wording discipline is right, but a single deck cannot serve both audiences well even with correct wording, because the *evidence* each buyer needs is structurally different: one wants numbers before a story, the other wants a story before numbers. **Concretely:** build two separate customer-facing pieces, not one deck reused for both —

1. A **B2B/procurement one-pager or sample-kit insert** — lab data first, spec table, ESG certification status including the honest end-of-life caveat, reference account once one exists. This is what [09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md](09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md) §11.2 is already describing; it just hasn't been named as distinct from the investor/business plan deck.
2. A **D2C/retail storefront and social content set** — photography-led, story-led, priced and positioned against premium comparables, with the honest sustainability claims stated plainly on the product page itself (§21.2.1).

Neither of these is the *investor* pitch deck this plan itself might eventually need (a third, separate artifact, out of scope for this doc) — the point is that "pitch deck" has been used loosely across this plan's docs to mean at least three different things aimed at three different audiences, and conflating them is a real risk once the team actually sits down to build sales material.

This split isn't just a messaging preference — it shows up as a real cost asymmetry. [20_Data_Metrics_Framework.md](20_Data_Metrics_Framework.md) §20.1.3 makes the same two-persona split from the acquisition-cost side: Channel 1 (this doc's §21.1) is hypothesized to run a *lower* CAC per account than Channel 2 (§21.2) precisely because it rides an existing technical-sales playbook and Sanjay's converter-shaped network, while Channel 2 needs brand-building spend this plan doesn't currently budget for. That doc's §20.4 also has the per-sector/per-SKU revenue-and-margin detail behind §21.4.1's account list — read it alongside this doc rather than re-deriving the numbers here.

## 21.4 Concrete next actions — validating these personas before Stage 1 capex

Nothing in §21.1–21.3 has been tested against a real buyer. [07_Capital_Plan.md](07_Capital_Plan.md) §9.2.4 already gates Stage 1 equipment ordering on 3–5 signed LOIs; this section is the customer-*understanding* work that should happen alongside — and largely before — chasing those signatures, so the LOIs are built on a correct read of what the buyer actually needs, not an assumed one.

**21.4.1 Who to talk to first — 5–10 named account types, drawn from [09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md](09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md) §11.1 and [13_Paper_Industry_Economics_Deep_Dive.md](13_Paper_Industry_Economics_Deep_Dive.md) §13.7's ranking:**

1. A mid-size packaging converter already serving an FMCG or pharma brand (§11.1 #1) — the highest-priority interview, since this is the lowest-CAC entry point in the existing plan.
2. A corporate-gifting or premium print house buyer (§11.1 #3, §13.7 #1) — the segment the plan itself says to lead with for revenue.
3. A pharma secondary-packaging buyer at a company in the Rajasthan/Gujarat pharma corridor (§13.7 #2).
4. An industrial labels/tags converter (§11.1 #4, §13.7 #3) — the highest-margin segment identified, worth checking whether the property pitch actually lands with this buyer as cleanly as the desk research assumes.
5. A premium D2C brand's packaging/ops lead (not a marketplace RFP contact) — tests the narrow e-commerce-mailer niche §13.7 #4 says is viable.
6. An individual retail shopper in the gifting-occasion window (wedding-season or festival-season buyer) — tests §21.2's D2C persona directly, cheaply, via informal conversation or a small test listing.
7. A small-business or corporate-gifting D2C buyer (CSR/onboarding-kit purchaser) — the hybrid persona at the bottom of §21.1's table.
8. Whitestone Stationeries or a similarly positioned account that reportedly sampled Ston2pack's product before it went dormant ([04_Competitive_Landscape.md](04_Competitive_Landscape.md) §5.4, [09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md](09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md) §11.2) — a warm lead who has already evaluated a stone-paper supplier once and can describe exactly what that evaluation looked like.
9. A school/institutional notebook buyer or a GeM-registered vendor contact (§11.1 #5, §13.7 #5) — lower priority, but worth one conversation to check whether the "18–24 month horizon" call in §13.7 is right.
10. An SPCB/RPCB contact or an existing marble-slurry disposal contractor in Kishangarh/Rajsamand — not a customer, but adjacent diligence that clarifies the feedstock-liability story this pitch leans on.

**21.4.2 Questions to actually ask — not to confirm the personas above, to break them:**

* "Walk me through the last time you qualified a new packaging substrate supplier — what happened, in order, and how long did it take?" (tests the sales-cycle-length assumption in §21.1 directly, rather than assuming it)
* "What would make you say no to a new supplier, even if the sample tested fine?" (surfaces objections not yet on the list in §21.1)
* "Who else has to sign off before you can place a pilot order?" (tests the assumed decision unit)
* "If I told you this material isn't biodegradable and doesn't recycle in standard streams, does that end the conversation, or is it one factor among several?" (directly tests §21.1.1's stakes — don't assume the answer, ask it)
* For the D2C persona: "What made you buy [comparable premium notebook/gift item] instead of the cheaper option — what were you actually paying for?" (tests §21.2's story-over-spec assumption)
* "Where did you first hear about [that comparable product]?" (tests the channel assumptions in §21.3 rather than assuming Instagram/IndiaMART as given)
* For both: "What information do you wish a new supplier/brand gave you upfront that they usually don't?" — an open question, deliberately not leading toward lab data or story, to catch anything this doc missed entirely.

**21.4.3 Sequencing.** Run these conversations in parallel with, not after, the prospect-list-building step already in [08_Implementation_Roadmap.md](08_Implementation_Roadmap.md) Phase E (step 23, target list of 20–30 B2B prospects) — the interview questions above should be asked of the same prospect list before or during the sample-and-LOI push (steps 24–25), not as a separate research exercise that delays it. For the D2C side, the cheapest form of validation is a small test listing or a handful of direct conversations before Phase F's storefront build (step 27) — even informal feedback on a mockup product page beats none. **This doc should be revised once 5–10 of these conversations actually happen** — expect at least one persona attribute above to be wrong.
