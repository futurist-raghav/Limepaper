# Unit Economics — Siting Near Kishangarh/Rajsamand, and vs. Wood-Pulp Paper

*Part of the Limepaper business plan — see [00_README_Index.md](00_README_Index.md).*

## 4.1 Why the location argument holds — and where it doesn't

Kishangarh ("Marble City of India") and Rajsamand sit at the centre of Rajasthan's marble/limestone belt, which also supplies large cement players (Shree, JK, Ambuja/UltraTech). Two facts matter:

* Marble slurry is a disposal liability, not just a cheap input. Kishangarh's dedicated slurry dumping yard (82 acres) takes a truck roughly every 10 minutes at peak, and CPCB/NGT have flagged groundwater/soil contamination — processors actively want offtake, so feedstock deals can plausibly be negotiated cheaply or "we-collect-it-for-you."
* Local pricing confirms the gap: raw marble slurry/dust trades ₹1,000–2,500/t (Kishangarh/Alwar/Makrana); locally milled micronized CaCO₃ (the finer grade stone paper needs) trades ₹3,600–9,500/t — vs. a global benchmark of USD 50–200 (≈₹4,750–19,000)/t for industrial-waste-grade CaCO₃. Rajasthan sourcing sits at or below the global low end.
* Counter-point: CaCO₃ is ~80% of finished mass but so cheap that HDPE resin (~18% of mass, USD 987–1,277 ≈ ₹94,000–121,000/t, sourced nationally, not local) is the larger raw-material line. Proximity to mines mainly compresses an already-small cost line; the bigger value is supply security, near-zero freight, and the ESG/circular-economy story below.
* **Caveat:** "cheap local limestone" assumes usable, on-spec limestone. Marble-processing slurry and cement-plant-adjacent CaCO₃ streams both carry impurities (mixed dust, kiln dust, silica, iron) and aren't usable at stone-paper grade (whiteness, particle size, low contaminants) without real purification/beneficiation. §4.2.1 prices this in rather than assuming it away.

Get firm quotes from Reliance/GAIL-linked distributors and 2–3 local micronizing units before underwriting a plan, specifically at 1,500–2,500 mesh / high-whiteness grade — raw marble slurry isn't directly usable without purification and fine grinding (§4.2.1).*

### 4.2.1 Refining/purification — the cost the ₹1,400–3,800 line hides

The CaCO₃ price range spans wide because the low end (≈₹1,000–2,500/t) is **unprocessed raw slurry, not usable as feedstock on its own.** The realistic input cost is the higher end — milled, micronized, purity-tested powder at ≈₹3,600–9,500/t — because getting there requires washing, fine grinding to 1,500–2,500 mesh, whiteness/purity testing, and rejecting contaminated batches.

Two paths, different cost profiles — full financial treatment in [07_Capital_Plan.md](07_Capital_Plan.md) §9.2.3:

* **Outsource** — buy already-purified powder from a local micronizing unit at ₹3,600–9,500/t. No separate capex.
* **In-house** — buy raw slurry cheap, add a beneficiation/grinding line. A real, separately-quoted capex decision, not a rounding error.

**Sourcing note:** marble-processing slurry and cement-plant-adjacent CaCO₃ streams aren't interchangeable — the latter can carry a different, less consistent contaminant profile. Lab-test every new source, including repeat batches from existing suppliers.

## 4.5 Rajasthan policy tailwinds

* **RIPS 2024–2029:** standard incentive package for new manufacturing — investment/capital subsidy, turnover-linked incentives, interest subsidy, MSME skill-development support. Confirm exact slabs with DIC/RIICO before finalizing.
* **Rajasthan Circular Economy Incentive Scheme 2025 — the standout fit.** Units where ≥51% (by weight) of raw material is recycled/reused waste qualify — a marble/limestone-slurry plant clears this easily (CaCO₃ alone is ~80% of input mass). Benefits: full RIPS-2024 access plus "thrust sector" status for the first 5 approved units, margin-money assistance of 25% (micro, up to ₹15L/yr) or 15% (small, up to ₹20L/yr) for the first 20 MSMEs (+₹5L for SC/ST/PwD or young entrepreneurs), 50% technology-acquisition assistance (up to ₹5L/yr) for the first 20 startups, and electricity duty exemptions.
* Several benefits are capped at "first 5" or "first 20" approved units — engage Rajasthan's Department of Industries early to check queue position.

## 4.6 Stone Paper vs. Wood-Pulp Paper — Full Unit Economics Comparison

The comparison worth underwriting a plan on: not "is stone paper cheap" alone, but how its resource-and-capital profile stacks up against the wood-pulp paper it displaces.

### 4.6.1 Per-tonne materials & energy cost

| Cost element | Wood-pulp paper (per tonne output) | Stone paper (per tonne output) | Notes |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Primary raw material | ~4t pulpwood @ ₹8,000–15,000/t = **₹32,000–60,000** | 0.80t CaCO₃ @ ₹1,750–4,750/t = **₹1,400–3,800** | Wood cost has surged ~88% (₹8,000→₹15,000/t) recently; India pays a premium (~USD50/t) vs. some international buyers (~USD30/t). |
| Binder / process chemicals | Folded into "chemicals," not broken out | 0.18t HDPE @ ₹94,000–121,000/t = **₹16,900–21,800** | HDPE is a national petrochemical commodity, not Rajasthan-sourced — stone paper's largest, most volatile cost line, not the mineral. |
| Additives / coating | included in chemicals line | 0.02t @ ~₹50,000–80,000/t = **₹1,000–1,600** | |
| **Raw-material subtotal** | **≈ ₹32,000–60,000+ (wood alone; chemicals extra, typically 5–10% of opex)** | **≈ ₹19,000–27,000** | Even with expensive HDPE, stone paper's material bill runs roughly half of wood-pulp's. |
| Process water | ~200 t/t, plus ETP capex/opex this volume mandates | ~0 | Biggest structural advantage for water-stressed Rajasthan — a siting and community-relations constraint stone paper skips entirely. |
| Energy | ~1,300–1,700 kWh/t electricity **plus** 11–15 t/t steam (coal/biomass boiler) — electricity alone ≈ **₹9,750–12,750** at ₹7.5/kWh, before steam cost | ~700 kWh/t, **no boiler/steam stage** ≈ **₹5,250** | Energy runs 16–25% of wood-pulp production cost industry-wide; stone paper skips pulping, bleaching and drying. |
| **Illustrative all-in (materials + energy only)** | **≈ ₹42,000–73,000+** | **≈ ₹24,000–32,000** | Excludes labour, maintenance, depreciation — roughly proportional for both, not broken out. |

*Stone paper's variable cost per tonne runs roughly 40–55% below wood-pulp — a genuinely different story from the "cheap limestone" framing in §4.1. The saving comes mostly from water and energy, and from wood being an increasingly expensive, partly-imported input. HDPE exposure is the one volatility risk stone paper inherits that wood-pulp doesn't.*

*For reference, India recycled-paper mills' reported opex split (raw materials 55–65%, utilities 20–25%, labour 5–8%, chemicals 5–10%, maintenance 3–5%, other 3–5%) is a useful sanity check, though on a different cost base (recycled fibre, not virgin pulpwood).*

### 4.6.2 Capex — a discrepancy worth flagging

An IndiaMART listing prices a 250 kg/hr stone-paper extrusion line (≈1,800 t/yr at full utilisation — comparable to the EIRI 5 TPD / ~1,400 t/yr benchmark) at **₹1.2 crore** — roughly **14x cheaper** than the ₹17.2 crore EIRI turnkey benchmark. Not a contradiction, a scope gap: ₹1.2cr is very likely the bare extrusion machine from one unverified listing, excluding land, building, micronizing/grinding, calendering/coating/finishing, utilities and commissioning — all of which the EIRI figure includes. **Treat ₹1.2cr as a "core machine only" floor and ₹17.2cr as the realistic turnkey number** until there's a firm, itemised, all-in quote ([06_Core_Team_Experts.md](06_Core_Team_Experts.md) role #7).

At ~5,000 t/yr, the global benchmark from §4.3 holds: a stone-paper plant (≈USD 6.37m/₹60.5cr) costs ~40% less to build than an equivalent wood-pulp mill (≈USD 10.6m/₹100cr) — capex, payback (2–3 yrs vs 4–6), and opex advantages (§4.6.1) all point the same direction. These benchmarks map onto the plan's two stages: Stage 1 is a bootstrapped starter line under a ₹1.5 crore borrowing ceiling; Stage 2 is the ₹15 crore equity raise sized against the EIRI 5 TPD turnkey benchmark (₹17.2cr) — see [07_Capital_Plan.md](07_Capital_Plan.md) §9.1 and §9.3. The ₹60.5cr global benchmark stays a market reference, not a target.

## 4.7 SKU-level economics and the two revenue channels

Everything above prices a **tonne**. A customer buys a hang tag, a carton blank, or a carry bag, not a tonne — [18_Product_SKU_Catalog.md](18_Product_SKU_Catalog.md) does that translation for the ten finalized SKUs (§18.11 cost roll-up), using one pricing rule for all of them: **price = substrate cost + min(cost × 70%, ₹2.00)** (§18.0.2). Don't re-derive per-SKU cost here — read it there; the tonne-level figures above are the input its formula uses.

Two revenue channels run off the same extrusion line, with different margin structures:

* **Raw substrate / roll & sheet supply to converters and brands** — the channel [09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md](09_B2B_Client_Acquisition.md) is built around. Thin per-unit margin, low conversion cost on our side (no die-cutting, binding, or finishing labour), volume is someone else's to sell through. This is §4.6's tonne-level economics applied almost directly.
* **Finished, converted goods under our own name** — notebooks, cartons, labels, bags — where we capture the converting margin the first channel gives away, at the cost of running converting equipment ([17_Factory_Operations_Plan.md](17_Factory_Operations_Plan.md) §17.2.2) and owning sales/brand ourselves. §18.11 is the per-SKU version of this margin.
* **In-house use** — not a revenue channel, but worth naming: office stationery, our own outbound-sample packaging, anything the plant itself consumes. Substrate-only cost (no markup, no sales effort), and where finished-goods quality gets proven on a forgiving captive customer before external sale.

Stage 1 runs channel 1 as the primary, LOI-gated revenue plan ([07_Capital_Plan.md](07_Capital_Plan.md) §9.2.4) because it needs the least converting capex to stand up. Channel 2 scales with how much of §17.2.2's converting equipment is actually bought and running — see that section for which SKUs are realistic to convert in-house at Stage 1 vs. which wait for volume or Stage 2.
